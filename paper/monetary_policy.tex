\begin{abstract}
I examine whether separating monetary-policy and information components changes the estimated response of German durable relative to non-durable consumer-goods production. On 308 monthly origins, matched local projections give a twelve-month policy-component contrast of $-2.29$ percentage log-change points per ten-basis-point factor contribution, compared with $-1.03$ for the recomposed surprise. The adjustment gap is $1.26$, with prespecified conservative 95 per cent bounds $[-0.11,3.31]$. Every declared sensitivity retains a positive point gap but an interval including zero. I apply an exact mixture decomposition and retain uncertainty in its estimated weight. An illustrative calculation omitting weight uncertainty excludes zero, whereas the prespecified population-gap interval includes zero. A durable-stock benchmark distinguishes user-cost and outlook channels; a measurement bridge states the extra conditions needed for causal interpretation. The application clarifies how announcement measurement and the uncertainty target affect the interpretation of production heterogeneity, conditional on the supplied shock construction and identifying assumptions.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textit{Keywords:} monetary policy; information shocks; consumer-goods production; local projections
\par\smallskip\noindent\textit{JEL classification:} C22, E32, E52.
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Monetary-policy announcements can change expectations about both financing conditions and the economic outlook \citep{JarocinskiKaradi2020}. An unexpected rise in near-term interest rates may signal tighter policy, more favourable information about activity, or a combination of the two. If the components have different effects on production, a regression on their combined rate surprise answers a different question from a regression that separates them. I examine how much this distinction changes the estimated response of German durable relative to non-durable consumer-goods production.

The economic motivation is heterogeneous transmission. A durable stock supplies services after purchase, allowing replacement expenditure to adjust more than current service consumption. The user cost of that stock depends on financing, expected resale prices and risk; outlook news can also change expenditure through income and wealth. The relative production response therefore need not have the same sign under policy and information contributions \citep{BarskyEtAl2007,DuranteEtAl2022,DedolaLippi2005}. Consequently, the apparent durability differential may depend on what the announcement variable measures. I compare the two interpretations on identical production outcomes, origin months, controls and rate units. The primary object is the difference between the recomposed-surprise coefficient and the policy-component coefficient at a fixed twelve-month horizon.

The matched comparison has an exact projection decomposition. Its coefficient gap equals a residualised information weight multiplied by the difference between the two component-response coefficients. The weight describes the mixture after conditioning on the selected controls; it is not a probability of an information announcement. For a population-projection comparison, it is also an estimated quantity. I therefore retain both component-response uncertainty and variation in this weight. Established partial-regression algebra supplies the identity; the project-specific application is its use to assess what information adjustment changes in the German production evidence.

The twelve-month policy-component contrast is $-2.29$ percentage log-change points per ten-basis-point factor contribution, compared with $-1.03$ for the recomposed surprise. The gap is $1.26$, with prespecified conservative 95 per cent bounds $[-0.11,3.31]$. Thus the point estimates suggest appreciably less relative contraction when the components are combined, but the prespecified conservative procedure does not reject a zero population gap. Every declared twelve-month sensitivity gives a positive point gap and an interval including zero. The earlier-period policy-contrast interval also includes zero. These findings constrain the conclusion rather than motivate selection of another horizon or covariance convention.

The uncertainty calculation matters numerically. Omitting weight-variation and covariance terms gives an illustrative normal interval excluding zero. The stacked population-gap diagnostic and primary conservative interval include zero. The arithmetic illustrates omitted terms rather than validating a conditional confidence interval. The zero-gap null is a union of zero-weight and zero-response-difference cases, so this non-rejection is specific to the declared conservative procedure. Nor does separate significance of the policy and recomposed coefficients establish a significant difference.

I contribute a focused reassessment of information adjustment in a carefully defined production contrast. Durability heterogeneity, information-adjusted monetary shocks and local projections are established in the literature. A newer German sample does not by itself establish novelty. The evidence here links the measured adjustment to its component-response difference, its mixture weight and the uncertainty target on the same design. Its economic interpretation remains conditional on the supplied announcement decomposition and the maintained projection assumptions.

\section{Economic rationale and related evidence}
\label{sec:economic-context}

\subsection{Durability, purchases and production}

A durable purchase adds to a stock that supplies services over time; expenditure and service consumption are distinct objects \citep{Mankiw1982,BarskyEtAl2007}. I use a simple benchmark to separate user-cost and demand channels, rather than to calibrate a German production elasticity. With non-durable consumption $C_t$ as numeraire, end-of-period stock $S_t$, purchases $X_t$ and depreciation $0<d\le1$,
\begin{equation}
 S_t=(1-d)S_{t-1}+X_t.\label{eq:stock-law}
\end{equation}
For an interior household with logarithmic utility weights $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$, the stock-service condition is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{S_t}{C_t}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha q_t},\qquad
 q_t=p_t-(1-d)\E_t[M_{t+1}p_{t+1}]>0.
 \label{eq:user-cost}
\end{equation}
Here $p_t$ is the durable price in non-durable units and $M_{t+1}$ the household's marginal-utility discount factor. User cost deducts the risk-adjusted retained resale value from today's price. Holding current and expected relative prices and the risk-adjustment term fixed, a higher real financing rate raises user cost when $d<1$; the financing effect vanishes at $d=1$. Expected resale prices and risk can offset the cost movement, while income and wealth can change the purchase response through $C_t$. Appendix~\ref{app:household} derives the conditions and states the required interiority, resale and timing assumptions.

At a stationary-stock base with positive replacement purchases $\bar X=d\bar S$, holding inherited stock fixed gives the local relation
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm d\log(X_t/C_t)=\left(\frac1d-1\right)\mathrm d\log C_t
                  -\frac1d\mathrm d\log q_t.
 \label{eq:relative-flow}
\end{equation}
The stock-flow ratio $1/d$ amplifies the purchase adjustment needed to change services. Conditional on current non-durable consumption, a user-cost increase reduces the relative purchase flow; favourable demand or wealth news can act in the opposite direction when $d<1$. These are local partial responses. Adjustment costs, borrowing constraints and endogenous relative prices can alter them. In the general-equilibrium models of \citet[pp.~990--992, 995]{BarskyEtAl2007}, which sector has sticky prices can even reverse durable co-movement with a monetary expansion. Their long-lived-durable argument does not supply an unconditional ordering for these production groups.\footnote{The simple US expenditure dynamics tested by \citet{Mankiw1982} are rejected; I use its stock-service distinction as a conceptual foundation, not empirical validation. The benchmark's real return is period-matched to depreciation. The empirical OIS factor is nominal and annualised, so inserting its ten-basis-point contribution into the household derivative would require an additional mapping.}

The mechanism motivates heterogeneity without predicting the sign of the information-component durable-minus-non-durable response. Favourable news can raise both sectors equally, or raise non-durable activity more. A positive adjustment gap requires an information response above the policy response when the weight is positive; it does not require a positive relative information response. \citet{DuranteEtAl2022} provide firm-level evidence of stronger investment and sales responses among durable producers, while the household benchmark explains one reason the purchase flow can be sensitive.

That mechanism does not translate directly into a household-demand elasticity estimated from production indices. German production serves both domestic and foreign purchasers. Inventory accumulation or depletion can separate the timing of sales from the timing of production. Firms' financing requirements can also change their capacity to maintain output independently of a household's decision to postpone a purchase. \citet{DedolaLippi2005} relate industrial responses to financing requirements, borrowing capacity and firm size as well as durability. The relative production coefficient therefore combines potentially different demand, financing and adjustment responses. I use durability to motivate the comparison while keeping these alternative explanations within its interpretation.

The classification makes this distinction material. I use Eurostat's official consumer-goods production groupings, which allocate industries at both division and group levels. Motor-vehicle manufacturing belongs to capital goods under the applicable classification, even though household purchases of cars can enter durable expenditure.\footnote{The production allocation is specified in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/1197, Annex II.A. The supply-use manual includes motor vehicles in household consumer durables \citep[pp.~151, 397]{Eurostat2008}. These are different measurements.} The non-durable production grouping likewise excludes household services. I therefore cannot transfer a result for broad durable manufacturing, investment goods or household consumption to these two production categories without checking what the comparison actually measures.

The closest published production studies already establish heterogeneous monetary transmission. \citet{PeersmanSmets2005} study eleven industries in seven euro-area countries, including Germany, over 1980--1998. Their durability classification includes capital and intermediate durables. \citet{DedolaLippi2005} examine twenty-one manufacturing sectors in five countries, including Germany, with domestic-policy identification. \citet{ChoiEtAl2024} subsequently use an annual international industry panel and interactions between monetary shocks and industry characteristics.\footnote{I consulted the published Peersman--Smets and Dedola--Lippi journal texts, including their identification and classification passages. For Choi et al., the full methods consultation uses the December 2023 update of Bank of England Staff Working Paper 1024 alongside the published introduction and preview; the final full methods text was not consulted.} Their differential approach also recognises that a common macroeconomic disturbance can have unequal sectoral effects. Subtracting the two production outcomes removes an identical common response, but does not remove a disturbance simply because it occurs in both sectors.

\subsection{Information adjustment and the economic comparison}

An interest-rate surprise around a policy announcement can convey more than an unexpected change in the policy stance. Under the interpretation developed by \citet{JarocinskiKaradi2020}, policy tightening raises the rate surprise while lowering equity prices, whereas favourable central-bank information can raise both. The latter component may affect expected activity differently from the policy component. A regression on their combined rate contribution can consequently describe a different production response from a regression that separates them. In this study, the same supplied factor provides the rate units for both comparisons; its contribution is not an equal-sized change in a particular ECB policy rate.

The component labels remain identifying interpretations. \citet{BauerSwanson2023a} show that surprises can reflect an unexpectedly strong systematic response to public economic news while retaining conventional rate--equity signs. This complicates both private-information interpretations and exogeneity of the policy-labelled component. Their evidence concerns Federal Reserve announcements and does not measure the magnitude of this issue in the German application. It nevertheless limits what rate-equity co-movement establishes. Their published monthly analysis also demonstrates why the information content of high-frequency surprises matters for subsequent macroeconomic estimation \citep{BauerSwanson2023b}. I examine sensitivity to the adopted decomposition without identifying the information contribution as superior private knowledge or its response as a separately measured expectations mechanism.

Information-adjusted durability comparisons are also established. \citet{DuranteEtAl2022} combine firms from four large euro-area economies, including Germany, with the original poor-man ECB proxy. Their annual investment and sales responses concern broader manufacturing groups. \citet{CasalisKrustev2022} compare quarterly durable and non-durable expenditure, including services in the latter, using a monetary-conditions shock that encompasses the broader lending environment.\footnote{For the consumption data and identification I consulted ECB Working Paper 2386, April 2020, alongside the published abstract and introduction; the final journal methods text was not consulted.} These comparisons motivate heterogeneity while differing in outcome, frequency, categories and intervention from the matched production comparison here.

The particularly close \citet{FlaccadoroRopele2026} already use monthly euro-area consumer-goods production, information-adjusted ECB shocks and local projections. Their twelve-month cross-industry durability comparison uses a broader classification, while their consumer-group mapping uses coarse two-digit industries rather than the detailed official allocation used here. This June 2026 working paper is an explicitly unpublished supplement; its consultation prevents treating the combination of production categories, updated shocks and local projections as a new contribution. I frame the present study as a focused economic reassessment: how much does the undivided-surprise coefficient differ from the policy-component coefficient when the outcome and design are held constant? The consulted literature does not establish that this exact contrast has never been studied.

\section{Data and measurement}
\label{sec:data_measurement}

I use German monthly production-volume indices for durable and non-durable consumer goods from Eurostat's \texttt{sts\_inpr\_m} dataset. Both indices are seasonally and calendar adjusted and referenced to 2021=100. They measure production in the official main industrial groupings (MIGs). These groupings combine NACE Rev.~2 divisions and groups, rather than assigning every two-digit manufacturing industry to a durability category \citep{EC2020,Eurostat2026}. Consumer durables comprise consumer electronics, optical and photographic equipment, domestic appliances, miscellaneous transport equipment, furniture, jewellery and musical instruments. Crucially, motor-vehicle manufacturing belongs to capital goods.\footnote{The durable grouping contains NACE Rev.~2 groups 26.4, 26.7, 27.5 and 30.9, division 31, and groups 32.1 and 32.2. Division 29, including motor vehicles, trailers and semi-trailers, is allocated to capital goods in Regulation 2020/1197, Annex II.A. Short-term statistics retain NACE Rev.~2 through 2027.} I therefore interpret the outcome as relative production in these defined manufacturing groupings. Exports, inventories and financing can influence production alongside domestic household demand; the indices do not separately identify these channels.

My price control uses Germany's unadjusted all-items HICP, \texttt{prc\_hicp\_midx}, in the historical ECOICOP classification, referenced to 2015=100. I construct annual logarithmic inflation as
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t=100\log\!\left(P_t/P_{t-12}\right).
  \label{eq:annual_inflation}
\end{equation}
The series ends in December 2025, but this does not restrict the primary response window. At the last primary origin, August 2025, the latest lagged price observation is July 2025. Future production is available through August 2026; future prices do not enter the control matrix. I retain a coherent historical price series throughout.\footnote{Eurostat has archived the historical classification and publishes a full-series ECOICOP version~2 successor, \texttt{prc\_hicp\_minr}. I do not splice these classifications. Calendar-month indicators accommodate a fixed seasonal pattern in the regressions; they do not turn HICP into an officially seasonally adjusted series.} Constant changes in index reference years cancel from the logarithmic changes used here.

The announcement variables are an author-maintained update of the policy and information decomposition introduced by \citet{JarocinskiKaradi2020}, using the EA-MPD monetary-event window of \citet{AltavillaEtAl2019}. I fix the update to its 7 January 2026 revision \citep{JarocinskiUpdate2026}. Its near-term rate factor is the first principal component of one-, three- and six-month and one-year OIS surprises. The four inputs are standardised by their sample standard deviations, without subtracting their sample means before component extraction. The component is then scaled to the standard deviation of the one-year OIS surprise and expressed in annualised percentage points. The accompanying equity surprise is 100 times the logarithmic change in the Euro Stoxx~50.

I use the supplied median admissible rotation of this rate--equity system. A common selected angle applies across announcements and permits policy and information contributions within the same event \citep{Jarocinski2022,JarocinskiUpdate2026}. Their sum reconstructs the rate factor. This is a later point-selected implementation, rather than the original article's monthly implementation.\footnote{For the later construction, the consulted version of \citet{Jarocinski2022} is the author manuscript dated 14 September 2022, \S\S2.1--2.2 and Appendix~B; I did not consult its final publisher text.} Event-column orthogonality is not preserved automatically by monthly aggregation, control residualisation or sample trimming.\footnote{The code's orthogonality concerns uncentred column products; it does not establish zero centred covariance or independence.} Consequently, it cannot justify suppressing joint policy--information dependence in the empirical comparison. I divide both contributions by 0.10, so a unit change represents ten basis points of the normalised factor contribution. This normalisation does not equate the estimates with the effect of an actual ten-basis-point ECB policy-rate increase.

I use the supplied monthly CSV contributions unchanged. Independent aggregation of the retained event columns reproduces them to the recorded numerical precision. I preserve supplied zero months and leave observations outside source coverage missing. After twelve lagged histories and endpoint requirements, the primary twelve-month comparison contains 308 origins, January 2000 to August 2025. The response path uses 296 common origins, ending August 2024. The fixed 10 October 2026 retrieval incorporates revised historical observations, rather than reconstructing information available at each announcement. Appendix~\ref{app:source_reconciliation} records source coverage and reconciliation. These checks establish measurement consistency; announcement identification and the interpretation of the selected components remain substantive assumptions.

\section{Matched empirical design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Outcome and announcement units}

Let $D_t$ and $N_t$ denote strictly positive durable and non-durable production indices. For each horizon $h$, I use
\begin{equation}
 Y_{t,h}=100\left\{\log\frac{D_{t+h}}{N_{t+h}}-\log\frac{D_{t-1}}{N_{t-1}}\right\}.
 \label{eq:outcome}
\end{equation}
This is the difference between the two cumulative percentage log changes, rather than a ratio of physical production quantities. It is invariant to separate constant rebasing of the indices. At $h=12$, the endpoint is one year after the announcement month relative to the preceding month, so the outcome sums thirteen monthly changes. I attribute the local-projection approach to \citet{Jorda2005}; the inference choices below rely on the consulted methods of \citet{StockWatson2018} and \citet{MontielOleaPlagborgMoller2021}.\footnote{I consulted Jord\`a's publisher metadata and abstract, rather than its original full methods text. The latter two sources were consulted in their published versions. For the lag-augmentation result I also consulted the authors' January 2026 correction \citep{MontielOleaPlagborgMoller2026}; I do not presume that my growth-control design inherits that theorem.}

Write $m_t=m_t^{\rm raw}/0.10$ and $c_t=c_t^{\rm raw}/0.10$ for the supplied policy and information contributions, and recompose $r_t=m_t+c_t$. All three regressors share rate-factor units. A unit is a ten-basis-point contribution to the normalised factor, not an observed policy-rate intervention of that size. I do not independently standardise the two components, since that would alter the comparison's units and its decomposition.

\subsection{Controls, sample and comparison}

I estimate the two ordinary least-squares projections
\begin{align}
 Y_{t,h}&=\beta_{m,h}m_t+\beta_{c,h}c_t+Z_t^\top\eta_h+e_{t,h},\label{eq:full}\\
 Y_{t,h}&=\gamma_h r_t+Z_t^\top\zeta_h+v_{t,h}.\label{eq:reduced}
\end{align}
The same rows and $Z_t$ enter both. They define linear-projection coefficients; the equations do not, by themselves, establish a correctly specified conditional mean or a structural intervention. A structural reading needs more. In a noiseless benchmark, let the supplied components $s_t=(m_t,c_t)^\top$ measure latent innovations $\varepsilon_t$ through a constant invertible matrix $A$, and let the structural relative response be $b_h$. Conditional orthogonality of the remaining production disturbance to the measured components then gives
\begin{equation}
 s_t=A\varepsilon_t,\qquad \beta_h=A^{-\top}b_h,
 \quad \beta_h=(\beta_{m,h},\beta_{c,h})^\top.
 \label{eq:measurement-bridge}
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:causal-bridge} proves this relation. A known diagonal mapping recovers the separately scaled latent responses. An unknown dense mapping mixes them; measurement noise generally breaks this identity. Orthogonal observed columns do not establish a diagonal mapping, exogeneity or response invariance. Rate--equity restrictions select an admissible interpretation of announcement news, rather than prove these conditions. I therefore estimate supplied-component projections and state the stronger assumptions needed to call them causal responses \citep{JarocinskiKaradi2020,StockWatson2018}.

The controls comprise an intercept, eleven calendar-month indicators and twelve lags of five histories: durable monthly log growth, non-durable monthly log growth, annual German HICP log inflation, and both scaled announcement components. I use preceding reference months, without contemporaneous production or inflation controls. Twelve lags allow a year of preceding dynamics, while the six-lag check assesses the parameter cost on unchanged dates. Month indicators allow residual calendar effects and announcement-opportunity patterns. These finite histories define the projection rather than demonstrate a sufficient information set or a maximum dependence length. Both models already condition on decomposed announcement histories: the primary gap isolates separation of the current contribution, conditional on those histories. It does not measure the total effect of abandoning information adjustment at every date.

The full primary regression has 74 columns and 234 residual degrees of freedom. Every origin requires its contemporaneous components, complete lagged controls, and positive production at $t-1$ and $t+h$. Future prices and shocks are not required. No missing observation is imputed and no primary pandemic origin is excluded. At $h=12$, the last available production endpoint implies August 2025 as the final origin, producing 308 monthly observations from January 2000. This is a historical projection over different monetary-policy regimes and pandemic states. A stable structural response across them needs stronger assumptions than the pooled specification.

I fixed the primary horizon, sample, outcome, units, controls and interval procedure before fitting the response models. Appendix~\ref{app:record} records chronology and the prespecified checks. The direct use of supplied contributions has no IV first stage. Small residual variation or influential announcements concern precision and numerical regularity here; they are not weak-instrument statistics \citep{StockWatson2018}.

The primary adjustment gap and the component-response difference are
\begin{equation}
 \delta_h=\gamma_h-\beta_{m,h},\qquad
 \theta_h=\beta_{c,h}-\beta_{m,h}.
 \label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
The policy coefficient is a companion estimand. Neither its individual interval nor the information coefficient's interval replaces an interval for $\delta_h$.

\begin{proposition}[Matched rate-surprise comparison]
\label{prop:mixture}
On identical rows, controls and units, let $Z$ have full column rank, $[m,c,Z]$ have full rank and $n>\operatorname{rank}[m,c,Z]$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\delta_h=\widehat w_{c,h}\widehat\theta_h,
 \qquad \widehat w_{c,h}=
 \frac{r^\top M_Zc}{r^\top M_Zr},\qquad
 M_Z=I-Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top.
 \label{eq:sample-gap}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

This application of the partial-regression result reviewed by \citet{Ding2021} is proved in Appendix~\ref{app:sample}. Its economic interpretation is straightforward. If the information weight is positive and the information response exceeds the policy response, combining the components shifts the estimated coefficient upward. When the policy response is negative, this makes the relative contraction appear smaller. But the weight need not lie in $[0,1]$: it reflects controlled covariation, rather than a probability or a variance share. Monthly aggregation, conditioning and horizon trimming can change that covariation even when the event-level source construction is orthogonal.

\section{Uncertainty in the adjustment gap}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

The finite-sample identity does not imply that its mixture weight should be treated as fixed for every inferential target. In the population projection, $w_{c,h}$ is a moment ratio of the rate and information residuals after projection on $Z$. Its estimated value varies with the regressor sample. Holding the supplied factor and selected rotation fixed is a different decision from holding realised regressor paths, or their mixture weight, fixed.

Under regular joint asymptotic linearity, the gap's influence satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \psi_{\delta,t}=w_c\psi_{\theta,t}+\theta\psi_{w,t},
 \label{eq:main-influence}
\end{equation}
with horizon subscripts suppressed. Thus its variance includes component-response variation, weight variation and their covariance. Appendix~\ref{app:population} derives each term, including the effect of residualising on estimated controls. Using only $|\widehat w_c|\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\theta)$ omits the latter two terms under alternatives; it does not establish conditional coverage by itself. At $w_c=0$ but $\theta\ne0$, weight variation is the leading first-order term. At $w_c=\theta=0$, the product gradient vanishes and ordinary gap-Wald inference is not generally regular.

I fit the full and reduced regressions with their own residuals, alongside the projection of $c$ on $[r,Z]$ that estimates $w_c$. I retain their joint coefficient contributions and cross-model covariance. The covariance uses Bartlett weights at actual calendar distances, bandwidth
\begin{equation}
 L_h=h+\left\lceil4(n/100)^{2/9}\right\rceil,
 \label{eq:main-bandwidth}
\end{equation}
and one common multiplier $n/(n-k_F)$, where $k_F$ is the full model's column count. At the primary horizon, $L_{12}=18$. The bandwidth grows with sample size; the correction is a finite-sample convention, rather than a guarantee of unbiased covariance or exact coverage. I use normal critical values and no prewhitening.

The procedure requires regular regressor moments, sufficient higher moments, a suitable weak-dependence central limit theorem and consistent long-run covariance estimation. The periodic calendar controls require the corresponding periodic mean or moment limits to be accommodated. Structural breaks, arbitrary trends and influential observations are not made harmless by naming HAC. Serially uncorrelated surprises also do not establish serially uncorrelated projection scores with overlapping outcomes. The lag-augmentation result of \citet{MontielOleaPlagborgMoller2021} has state-lag and innovation assumptions that my constructed-shock design does not establish automatically.

For the primary comparison I use a conservative product interval. I form individual 97.5 per cent normal intervals for $w_c$ and $\theta$, with critical value $z_{0.9875}$, and take the smallest and largest of their four endpoint products. The Bonferroni union bound gives asymptotic joint coordinate coverage of at least 95 per cent; mapping that rectangle covers their product. Appendix~\ref{app:covariance} proves the coverage statement. It avoids requiring an invertible joint covariance or a regular product gradient, while retaining the need for valid, nondegenerate marginal inference. I do not clip weights to $[0,1]$. The null $\delta_h=0$ is the union of $w_{c,h}=0$ and $\theta_h=0$. The declared rectangle is deliberately conservative; its non-rejection does not imply agreement among all valid zero-gap tests. Under a maintained nonzero weight, a restricted-null test of $\theta_h=0$ has a different justification. I retain the preselected product procedure rather than add a test after inspecting the estimates.

Individual response intervals and the normal stacked-gap interval remain pointwise; the latter is a regularity-dependent diagnostic. Neither supplies simultaneous coverage over a response path. Ordinary uncertainty conditions on the supplied factor, rotation, measurement vintage and specification. It does not incorporate their construction or identification-set uncertainty.

The undivided-surprise coefficient is $-1.03$ and its interval includes zero. Its difference from the policy coefficient is $1.26$, but the primary conservative bounds $[-0.11,3.31]$ also include zero. The prespecified conservative procedure therefore does not reject the zero-gap hypothesis. Non-rejection is not evidence that the adjustment has no economically relevant effect.

The decomposition attributes the point gap to an information weight of $0.427$ and component-response difference of $2.951$. Their product is $1.261$. The 97.5 per cent coordinate bounds are $[0.312,0.542]$ for the weight and $[-0.198,6.100]$ for the response difference; the latter permits the negative endpoint of the primary product bounds. This identifies how the coefficient comparison is assembled, rather than measuring the fraction of announcements containing information or identifying a distinct expectations channel. The normal component-difference interval alone cannot establish a nonzero product, and differing separate significance labels do not test the gap.

\subsection{An illustration of omitted variance terms}

Multiplying the component-difference standard error by the realised weight gives a standard error of $0.6005$ and an illustrative normal interval $[0.08,2.44]$. The primary conservative interval remains $[-0.11,3.31]$.

The three estimated sampling-variance contributions are approximately $0.3606$ from component-response uncertainty, $0.0229$ from weight variation and $0.0719$ from their cross covariance, summing to $0.4554$. Direct weight variation contributes about 5.0 per cent and its cross term about 15.8 per cent of the total; together they are the 20.8 per cent omitted by the illustrative arithmetic. Their sign and importance are sample-specific, rather than a universal upward correction. A valid fixed-design analysis requires its own stochastic model. Dropping terms from the marginal random-design variance does not validate such a conditional interval. It cannot substitute for the locked population comparison. This arithmetic illustration applies the established identity to the retained evidence; it neither changes the primary model nor supplies a new theoretical inference result.

\section{Economic interpretation and limitations}
\label{sec:interpretation}

The companion estimate is consistent with a larger contraction in the durable production group following a positive supplied policy contribution. Its comparison with the undivided rate surprise suggests a material role for how announcement news is measured. But the primary interval leaves uncertainty about whether this adjustment differs from zero in the population projection. The theory distinguishes user-cost, wealth and resale-price channels, but production data do not isolate them. The causal bridge further shows why the measured response depends on the mapping from announcement factors to latent innovations.

The difference outcome focuses on heterogeneity but does not automatically remove macroeconomic confounding. An energy-price, foreign-demand or supply disturbance with unequal loadings on the two groups can survive subtraction. Controlling the supplied information component does not demonstrate that the remaining policy component is exogenous. Likewise, an unexpectedly strong systematic response to public news can contaminate the policy-labelled contribution while retaining conventional rate--equity signs \citep{BauerSwanson2023a}. The maintained announcement interpretation is therefore economically substantive, beyond the numerical sign restrictions and regression identities.

The production allocation also constrains what the estimate can explain. A relative movement in these indices is not a household-expenditure elasticity, a motor-vehicle response or a change in the physical share of durable goods. Inventory, export demand, financing and demand postponement are possible mechanisms. The regressions do not separate them. The point estimates should be read in the source's normalised factor units, not translated into the effect of an actual policy-rate step without an additional normalisation and identification argument.

Finally, the finite sample averages over unconventional-policy periods, later tightening and pandemic states. A pooled linear coefficient need not describe an invariant structural response. Revised historical controls are not the real-time information set; the selected source factor and rotation also use their own construction sample. Independent numerical agreement validates implementation, not these economic assumptions. The combination of imprecise gaps, influential origins and unpropagated identification uncertainty limits a stronger causal claim.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

I compare information-adjusted and recomposed ECB rate-surprise responses on matched German consumer-goods production data. At twelve months, separating the supplied policy component yields a larger negative durable-minus-non-durable point response. The resulting adjustment gap is positive but imprecisely estimated: its primary conservative interval and every declared sensitivity interval include zero. The earlier-period comparison also weakens the companion policy-response inference.

The project-specific finding concerns both measurement and uncertainty. The same rate-factor units and projection design make the coefficient gap interpretable, while its estimated mixture weight enters the population variance together with cross covariance. Omitting those terms changes whether an illustrative arithmetic interval contains zero. The declared conservative procedure remains the primary comparison. The durable-stock argument explains a conditional purchase mechanism; relative-price feedback and the production measurement prevent treating it as a calibrated causal account of the estimates. It provides a reproducible economic reassessment whose conclusions follow from the defined contrast and retained uncertainty.

\section{Projection identities and uncertainty}
\label{app:projection}

The following derivations apply established partial-regression and moment-equation algebra to my matched comparison. \citet[Sections 2--3 and Appendix A]{Ding2021} provides the relevant published partial-regression and covariance results. I hold the supplied shock construction fixed while allowing sampling variation in the regressors and their projection weights.

\subsection{The exact sample comparison}
\label{app:sample}

Fix a horizon and suppress its subscript. Let $Y,m,c\in\mathbb R^n$ refer to identical observations, with $r=m+c$ in common units. The $n\times q$ control matrix $Z$ includes an intercept. I require $\operatorname{rank}(Z)=q$, $\operatorname{rank}[Z,m,c]=q+2$ and $n>q+2$. Define
\begin{align}
 M_Z&=I-Z(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top,
 & y&=M_ZY,\notag\\
 a&=M_Zm,
 & b&=M_Zc,\qquad s=a+b.\label{eq:app-residualise}
\end{align}
Thus $M_Z^\top=M_Z$, $M_Z^2=M_Z$ and $M_ZZ=0$. The residualised matrix $A=[a,b]$ has rank two, so $A^\top A$ is positive definite and $s^\top s>0$.

The identity stated in Proposition~\ref{prop:mixture} is derived as follows.
\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:mixture}]
For fixed $\beta=(\beta_m,\beta_c)^\top$, minimising the full criterion over its nuisance coefficient gives
\begin{align}
 \widehat\eta(\beta)&=(Z^\top Z)^{-1}Z^\top(Y-m\beta_m-c\beta_c),\\
 \min_\eta\|Y-m\beta_m-c\beta_c-Z\eta\|^2
   &=\|y-A\beta\|^2.\label{eq:app-profile}
\end{align}
Consequently $\widehat\beta=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top y$. The residual $\widehat e=y-A\widehat\beta$ satisfies $a^\top\widehat e=b^\top\widehat e=0$. Applying the same minimisation to the reduced regression gives
\begin{align}
 \widehat\gamma
 &=\frac{s^\top y}{s^\top s}
  =\frac{s^\top a}{s^\top s}\widehat\beta_m
   +\frac{s^\top b}{s^\top s}\widehat\beta_c,\qquad
 \frac{s^\top a+s^\top b}{s^\top s}=1.\label{eq:app-normal-equations}
\end{align}
Subtracting $\widehat\beta_m$ proves \eqref{eq:sample-gap}.
\end{proof}

This is an identity for every $Y$, without probabilistic assumptions. It does not survive different rows, controls, scaling or a different definition of $r$. Expanding its weight shows why it need not be a probability or variance share:
\begin{align}
 \widehat w_c
 &=\frac{b^\top b+a^\top b}{a^\top a+b^\top b+2a^\top b}.\label{eq:app-nonconvex}
\end{align}
Negative weights or weights above one are possible. If $\widehat w_c=0$, the sample gap is zero for every outcome although $\widehat\theta$ need not vanish. Event-level orthogonality also does not imply controlled monthly orthogonality: for an aggregation matrix $H$, the relevant inner product is $m_e^\top H^\top M_ZHc_e$, rather than $m_e^\top c_e$.

Since $m=r-c$, the full regression can instead be written $Y=\beta_m r+\theta c+Z\eta+e$. Its first two coefficients are the nonsingular transformation $T\beta$, where $T=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Fitted values and residuals are unchanged, and a consistently computed covariance transforms as $\widehat V_{\beta_m,\theta}=T\widehat V_{\beta_m,\beta_c}T^\top$.

\subsection{Population weights and their influence}
\label{app:population}

Let the corresponding population projections exist with nonsingular moment matrices. Expectations below use the stable population moments, or their periodically structured limits, required by the design. A tilde denotes population linear residualisation on $Z$. Write $\widetilde r=r-Z^\top\pi_r$ and $\widetilde c=c-Z^\top\pi_c$, where $\mathbb E[Z\widetilde r]=\mathbb E[Z\widetilde c]=0$. Put
\begin{align}
 B&=\mathbb E[\widetilde r^2]>0,
 & C&=\mathbb E[\widetilde r\widetilde c],
 & w_c&=C/B, &\theta&=\beta_c-\beta_m.\label{eq:app-population-weight}
\end{align}
For the full residual $e$, the projection normal equations give $\mathbb E[Ze]=\mathbb E[me]=\mathbb E[ce]=0$. Therefore $\gamma=B^{-1}\mathbb E[\widetilde r\widetilde Y]=\beta_m+w_c\theta$. The reduced residual consequently differs from the full residual:
\begin{align}
 \widetilde Y&=\beta_m\widetilde r+\theta\widetilde c+e,
 & v&=e+\theta(\widetilde c-w_c\widetilde r).\label{eq:app-reduced-residual}
\end{align}

The nuisance projections do not add first-order terms to $C$ or $B$, because
\begin{align}
 \partial_{\pi_r}C&=-\mathbb E[Z\widetilde c]=0,
 &\partial_{\pi_c}C&=-\mathbb E[Z\widetilde r]=0,
 &\partial_{\pi_r}B&=-2\mathbb E[Z\widetilde r]=0.\label{eq:app-nuisance}
\end{align}
Thus, under regular asymptotic linearity, their moment influences are $\widetilde r_t\widetilde c_t-C$ and $\widetilde r_t^2-B$. Differentiating their ratio gives
\begin{align}
 \psi_{w,t}
 &=\frac{(\widetilde r_t\widetilde c_t-C)-w_c(\widetilde r_t^2-B)}{B}
  =\frac{\widetilde r_t(\widetilde c_t-w_c\widetilde r_t)}{B}.\label{eq:app-weight-influence}
\end{align}

For $\xi_t=(\widetilde m_t,\widetilde c_t)^\top$ and $Q=\mathbb E[\xi_t\xi_t^\top]$, the full coefficient influence is $\psi_{\beta,t}=Q^{-1}\xi_t e_t$, while $\psi_{\gamma,t}=\widetilde r_t v_t/B$. Define $\psi_{\theta,t}=(-1,1)\psi_{\beta,t}$. The row identity $(1-w_c,w_c)=\mathbb E[\widetilde r\xi^\top]/B$ implies
\begin{align}
 \frac{\widetilde r_t e_t}{B}
  &=(1-w_c,w_c)\psi_{\beta,t}
    =\psi_{\beta_m,t}+w_c\psi_{\theta,t},\\
 \psi_{\delta,t}
  &=\psi_{\gamma,t}-\psi_{\beta_m,t}
    =w_c\psi_{\theta,t}+\theta\psi_{w,t}.\label{eq:app-gap-influence}
\end{align}
Hence the long-run variance contains
\begin{align}
 \mathcal V_\delta
 &=w_c^2\mathcal V_\theta+\theta^2\mathcal V_w
   +2w_c\theta\mathcal C_{\theta,w},\label{eq:app-gap-variance}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal C_{\theta,w}$ is the long-run covariance. A fixed-weight propagation retains only the first term. Under a genuinely fixed design $\mathcal X$, the sample identity implies $\Var(\widehat\delta\mid\mathcal X)=\widehat w_c^2\Var(\widehat\theta\mid\mathcal X)$. A marginal random-design HAC variance need not estimate that conditional variance. Conditioning only on an estimated weight is also different from fixing the complete design. If $w_c=0$ and $\theta\ne0$, weight variation is instead the leading term. If $\theta=0$ and $w_c\ne0$, the weight term vanishes under the null; this does not invalidate a response-difference test under a maintained nonzero weight. If both vanish, the first-order product gradient is zero and ordinary gap-Wald inference is not generally regular.

\subsection{Joint covariance and conservative product bounds}
\label{app:covariance}

I fit the full, reduced and weight regressions, respectively, with $X_F=[m,c,Z]$, $X_R=[r,Z]$ and $X_W=X_R$, using responses $Y,Y,c$ and each regression's own residual $\widehat u_{j,t}$. Let $\ell_{j,t}$ be column $t$ of $L_j=(X_j^\top X_j)^{-1}X_j^\top$. The distinction between the population-scale influence and the numerical coefficient contribution is
\begin{align}
 \widehat\psi_{j,t}
 &=\widehat Q_j^{-1}x_{j,t}\widehat u_{j,t}
   =n\ell_{j,t}\widehat u_{j,t},
 &\widehat Q_j&=X_j^\top X_j/n,
 &a_{j,t}&=\widehat\psi_{j,t}/n.\label{eq:app-contribution}
\end{align}
I compute $L_j$ through the full-rank QR factorisation. Let $a_t$ collect the contributions for $\beta_m$, $\beta_c$, $\gamma$ and $w_c$. It satisfies $a_{\delta,t}=a_{\gamma,t}-a_{\beta_m,t}=\widehat w_c a_{\theta,t}+\widehat\theta a_{w,t}$. Its four-dimensional first-order covariance is therefore generally singular; no rank-four inversion is required.

For origin-month ordinals $\tau_i$, the calendar Bartlett covariance is
\begin{align}
 \widehat V
 &=\kappa\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
       K_{L_h}(|\tau_i-\tau_j|)a_i a_j^\top,
 &\kappa&=\frac{n}{n-k_F},\qquad k_F=q+2,\label{eq:app-hac}\\
 K_L(d)&=\max\{1-d/(L+1),0\},
 &L_h&=h+\left\lceil4(n/100)^{2/9}\right\rceil.\label{eq:app-kernel}
\end{align}
Equivalently, \eqref{eq:app-hac} uses $\widehat\psi_i\widehat\psi_j^\top/n^2$. Calendar gaps are retained, not compressed. I apply the same correction to every joint component, without prewhitening. This finite-sample convention does not establish exact coverage. The inference requires a joint score central limit theorem, adequate moments and consistent long-run covariance estimation; lagged growth controls alone do not supply these conditions.

A fixed linear transformation of $\widehat V$ yields the covariance of $(\widehat\beta_m,\widehat\delta,\widehat w_c,\widehat\theta)$, retaining its cross-model blocks. Likewise, shared sector regressions obey $\widehat\beta_m=\widehat\beta_{D,m}-\widehat\beta_{N,m}$ and
\begin{align}
 \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat\beta_m)
 &=\widehat V_{D,m}+\widehat V_{N,m}
       -2\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\widehat\beta_{D,m},\widehat\beta_{N,m}).\label{eq:app-sector-covariance}
\end{align}
This covariance cannot be inferred from separate significance labels.

\begin{proposition}[Conservative product interval]
\label{prop:app-product}
Suppose $I_w=[w_-,w_+]$ and $I_\theta=[\theta_-,\theta_+]$ each cover their population coordinate with asymptotic probability at least $1-\alpha/2$. Then
\begin{align}
 I_\delta&=\left[
 \min_{s,t\in\{-,+\}}w_s\theta_t,
 \max_{s,t\in\{-,+\}}w_s\theta_t\right]\label{eq:app-product-bounds}
\end{align}
has asymptotic coverage at least $1-\alpha$ for $\delta=w_c\theta$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The product's extrema over the coordinate rectangle occur at its corners, since it is linear in either coordinate when the other is fixed. Therefore
\begin{align}
 \Pr\{\delta\in I_\delta\}
 &\ge\Pr\{w_c\in I_w,\theta\in I_\theta\}\notag\\
 &\ge1-\Pr\{w_c\notin I_w\}-\Pr\{\theta\notin I_\theta\}.
\end{align}
Taking the lower limit gives the claimed coverage without independence or an invertible joint covariance.
\end{proof}

I use normal marginal intervals with critical value $z_{1-\alpha/4}$, so at $\alpha=0.05$ each coordinate interval is 97.5 per cent. Weights are not restricted to $[0,1]$. The argument applies even at $w_c=\theta=0$ provided the coordinates themselves admit valid marginal inference with nondegenerate scalar long-run variances. It does not cure marginal degeneracy. The bounds remain asymptotic and pointwise in the horizon. Neither they nor numerical replication establish announcement exogeneity or incorporate uncertainty in the supplied factor loadings, rotation or vintage.

\subsection{Durable services, user cost and purchase adjustment}
\label{app:household}

I derive a partial household benchmark using the established stock-service distinction of \citet[pp.~418--420]{Mankiw1982} and \citet[pp.~985--986, 990--992]{BarskyEtAl2007}; it is not a new durability theorem. Let $0<\alpha<1$, $0<\beta_H<1$, $0<d\le1$, $p_t>0$ and $\rho_t>-1$. Consumption and stock are positive; purchases and bond holdings are interior, resale is allowed, and feasibility and transversality hold. There are no adjustment costs. With the stock law \eqref{eq:stock-law}, the household chooses consumption, stock and a bond with next-period face-value payoff $B_t$ to maximise
\begin{align}
 \max_{\{C_t,S_t,B_t\}}\quad
 &\E_0\sum_{t\ge0}\beta_H^t\{\alpha\log C_t+(1-\alpha)\log S_t\},\notag\\
 \text{subject to}\quad
 &C_t+p_tX_t+\frac{B_t}{1+\rho_t}=W_t+B_{t-1},\quad t\ge0.
 \label{eq:household-problem}
\end{align}
Resources $W_t$ are in non-durable units. The real risk-free return $\rho_t$, depreciation and discount factor refer to the same period. This return differs from the nominal recomposed announcement factor $r_t$.

Let $\lambda_t=\alpha/C_t$. Varying the bond and end-of-period stock gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\lambda_t}{1+\rho_t}&=\beta_H\E_t\lambda_{t+1},\label{eq:bond-euler}\\
 \lambda_tp_t&=\frac{1-\alpha}{S_t}
      +\beta_H(1-d)\E_t[\lambda_{t+1}p_{t+1}].\label{eq:stock-euler}
\end{align}
The stock condition values current services and the retained stock's next-period resale value. Set $M_{t+1}=\beta_H\lambda_{t+1}/\lambda_t$. Dividing \eqref{eq:stock-euler} by $\lambda_t$ proves \eqref{eq:user-cost}, including positive $q_t$. The bond condition and covariance decomposition then imply
\begin{equation}
 q_t=p_t-\frac{1-d}{1+\rho_t}\E_tp_{t+1}
             -(1-d)\Cov_t(M_{t+1},p_{t+1}).
 \label{eq:risky-user-cost}
\end{equation}
A negative covariance raises user cost relative to the expectation-only expression: resale is less valuable in high-marginal-utility states. Both this risk term and expected prices can change with announcement news.\footnote{This household valuation covariance differs from the observed announcement-time rate--equity covariance; it is not estimated by the production regressions.}

Under certainty and constant relative prices, $q_t=p(\rho_t+d)/(1+\rho_t)$, requiring $\rho_t+d>0$. This simplification does not require stationary consumption. Stationary consumption at a benchmark would additionally require $\beta_H(1+\bar\rho)=1$. At a stationary-stock base, $\bar X=d\bar S>0$. Holding inherited stock fixed gives $\mathrm dX_t=\mathrm dS_t$ and hence
\begin{align}
 \mathrm d\log X_t&=\frac1d\mathrm d\log S_t
   =\frac1d(\mathrm d\log C_t-\mathrm d\log q_t),\label{eq:flow-proof}\\
 \left.\frac{\partial\log X_t}{\partial\rho_t}\right|_{C_t,S_{t-1},p}
   &=-\frac{1-d}{d(1+\rho_t)(\rho_t+d)}.\label{eq:rate-flow-derivative}
\end{align}
Subtracting $\mathrm d\log C_t$ proves \eqref{eq:relative-flow}. The rate derivative is negative for $d<1$ and zero at $d=1$. It is a local impact comparison with positive purchases, not a comparison of steady states holding consumption fixed as rates vary. Zero depreciation, a binding no-resale constraint, a finite movement outside the positive-purchase neighbourhood or adjustment costs can invalidate this derivative. Endogenous consumption and prices instead alter the total equilibrium response. The positive-purchase restriction is material: with current $C_t$ fixed,
\begin{equation}
 X_t(q_t)=\bar S\left\{\frac{\bar q}{q_t}-(1-d)\right\}>0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q_t<\frac{\bar q}{1-d},\qquad 0<d<1.
 \label{eq:purchase-neighbourhood}
\end{equation}
As $d$ approaches zero, the amplification grows but this admissible contraction neighbourhood shrinks; a large finite elasticity does not follow from the local derivative. Exports, inventories, firm financing and index aggregation also separate household purchases from measured production.

\subsection{A conditional bridge from components to causal responses}
\label{app:causal-bridge}

For a fixed horizon, suppose a stable linear structural relation and a noiseless measurement equation hold:
\begin{equation}
 Y_{t,h}=b_h^\top\varepsilon_t+Z_t^\top\eta_h+\nu_{t,h},
 \qquad s_t=A\varepsilon_t.
 \label{eq:structural-measurement}
\end{equation}
The constant $A$ is invertible and $Q_s=\E[\widetilde s_t\widetilde s_t^\top]$ is positive definite. Tildes denote population linear residualisation on the same $Z_t$. Maintain $\E[\widetilde s_t\nu_{t,h}]=0$, which implies orthogonality to the residualised disturbance. Since $\widetilde\varepsilon_t=A^{-1}\widetilde s_t$,
\begin{equation}
 \beta_h=Q_s^{-1}\E[\widetilde s_t\widetilde Y_{t,h}]
        =Q_s^{-1}Q_sA^{-\top}b_h=A^{-\top}b_h.
 \label{eq:bridge-proof}
\end{equation}
If $A=\operatorname{diag}(a_m,a_c)$ is known with ordered nonzero scales, then $b_{m,h}=a_m\beta_{m,h}$ and $b_{c,h}=a_c\beta_{c,h}$. A known dense mapping also permits joint recovery $b_h=A^\top\beta_h$; a diagonal mapping is needed to read each supplied coefficient as one separately scaled latent response. An unidentified dense $A$ mixes responses. Orthogonality of observed columns constrains their second moments, not the mapping's diagonality or causal exogeneity. Noise in $s_t$ generally breaks \eqref{eq:bridge-proof}. A causal interpretation requires the structural relation, invariance and orthogonality in addition to matched regression rows and financial sign restrictions. The supplied point rotation and HAC intervals do not establish these assumptions or cover its entire identification set. Here $b_h$ is a difference of latent sector responses, not an intervention on a physical production ratio.
